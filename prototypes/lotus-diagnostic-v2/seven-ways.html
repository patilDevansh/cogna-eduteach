Seven Ways to Ask

Seven question formats for the factorisation check where the motion is the maths, not decoration. Each one records more than a final answer: how a student splits their certainty, the order they search in, the moment they spot an error, and the slip they fixed on their own. Watch the evidence feed on the right fill up as you play. The round print above it replaces the frog. It grows from what Lotus learns, never from right or wrong (see how it works). Below the studio, twelve blind spots list the ways a right answer can hide wrong thinking. Five of them have playable fixes, at the end of the row of prototypes. Sixteen more go deeper (effort, language, foundations, and the health of the questions themselves), followed by six changes that would make Lotus sharper, in the order to build them.

      Twelve ways an answer misleads

      A right answer isn't proof of right thinking, and a wrong one isn't proof of a misconception. These are the cases where marking only the final answer gets the student wrong, each with a factorisation example and how Lotus can catch it. Five have new playable fixes in the studio above. The rest need a change to how Lotus reads evidence, not a new question.

      Two wrongs make a right

      01

      For x² − x − 6: picks 3 and −2 (they add to +1, a sign slip), then flips the signs into the brackets: (x − 3)(x + 2). Correct.

      Answer-only marking sees
      A right answer. These two slips always cancel, so no amount of final-answer questions will ever catch them.
      Catch it by
      Recording the middle step. It breaks later, when they split the middle term in Class 9.

      A wrong rule that happens to work

      02

      "Both brackets copy the middle sign" gets x² + 5x + 6 and x² − 5x + 6 right, and fails every time the last number is negative.

      Answer-only marking sees
      Mastery, if every question it asked had a positive last number.
      Catch it by
      Covering every sign case, and choosing numbers where the wrong rules disagree with the right one.

      Solving the options, not the question

      03

      On a multiple-choice factorise question, the student expands each option until one matches. Expanding is a different, easier skill.

      Answer-only marking sees
      Factorising they can't yet do.
      Catch it by
      Asking for a prediction before the options appear, then comparing.

      Autopilot from the last question

      04

      After three differences of squares, x² + 16 gets (x − 4)(x + 4) in two seconds.

      Answer-only marking sees
      A run of right answers that were pattern-copying, not choosing a method.
      Catch it by
      Mixing question types and planting a look-alike. Timing shows whether they noticed.

      The mistake they fixed before submitting

      05

      Types (x − 5)(x + 2), pauses, deletes it, and submits (x + 5)(x − 2).

      Answer-only marking sees
      A clean right answer. The sign mistake is still there. They just caught it this time.
      Catch it by
      Keeping the edit history and checking deleted drafts against known mistakes.

      Same right answer, very different fluency

      06

      One student cracks x² − x − 12 in one try. Another takes nine scattered tries.

      Answer-only marking sees
      Two identical ticks.
      Catch it by
      Recording tries and time. Fluency counts as evidence, separately from correctness.

      A slip that looks like a misconception

      07

      Writes (x + 2)(x + 4) for x² + 5x + 6 once, and never again.

      Answer-only marking sees
      A gap, if it treats one wrong answer as a diagnosis.
      Catch it by
      Naming a mistake only when it shows up again, from a different angle.

      The mistake that changes shape

      08

      Monday: (x − 3)(x + 2). Wednesday: (x + 3)(x + 2). Different wrong answers, but the same missing piece: not knowing what the minus in −6 means.

      Answer-only marking sees
      Random slips, because the wrong answers keep changing.
      Catch it by
      Grouping wrong answers by the missing idea behind them, not by how they look (VanLehn's "bug migration").
      Change to how Lotus reads evidence

      Steps given, plan missing

      09

      "First take out the common factor, then factorise 2x² − 18." Gets it. Without the hint, they stop at 2(x² − 9).

      Answer-only marking sees
      Success that came from the hint.
      Catch it by
      Asking "what would you do first?" before any steps, and giving hints only after a miss.
      Change to the question order

      Knows the symbols, not the meaning

      10

      Factorises x² + 5x + 6 fine, but can't say which rectangle it is, or cancels the x's in (x² + 5x)/x term by term.

      Answer-only marking sees
      Mastery of a procedure, which isn't the same as understanding.
      Catch it by
      Asking the same idea in a second form: tiles, area, or a fraction to simplify.

      Remembering the textbook example

      11

      x² + 5x + 6 is in every textbook. 6 + y² + 5y is the same question in disguise. Only the skill transfers. A memorised example doesn't.

      Answer-only marking sees
      Recall of a familiar question, not the skill.
      Catch it by
      Varying the surface in code: another letter, terms in a different order, the same numbers in disguise.
      Change to how questions are written

      Right here, wrong as a rule

      12

      Says (x + 3)² = x² + 9 is true after trying only x = 0, the one value where it is.

      Answer-only marking sees
      A correct check on one case.
      Catch it by
      Recording which cases they tested before deciding.

      Research behind this: Brown & VanLehn's repair theory and "bug migration" (mistakes change shape between tests); Tatsuoka's rule-space work (read patterns across many answers, because one answer can come from a wrong rule); Rohrer's interleaving studies (mixed practice makes students choose a method; blocked practice lets them skip choosing); Imran & Bulathwela's "correct answer trap" (flawed reasoning hidden behind right answers in Eedi data); and keystroke and process-data research (PISA, EDM 2026), which treats revisions and pauses as evidence.

      Sixteen more, from deeper down

      The first twelve are about the maths in the answer. These go further: whether the student was really trying, whether the screen or the language got in the way, whether the real gap sits below factorising, and whether the question itself was fair, healthy and worth asking. Four have new playable prototypes, at the end of the row above.

      The student, not the maths effort, gaming, confidence, fatigue

      Not really trying

      13

      A factorise question answered in 1.4 seconds. Nobody can read and solve it that fast.

      Answer-only marking sees
      A wrong answer, and a gap that may not exist.
      Catch it by
      Setting a minimum believable time for each question. Faster answers count as rapid guesses: left out of the diagnosis, followed by a gentle "take your time" (Wise & Kong's response-time effort).
      Change to how Lotus reads evidence

      Gaming "I don't know"

      14

      A student learns that "I don't know yet" brings easier questions, and taps it within three seconds every time.

      Answer-only marking sees
      Lots of honest gaps.
      Catch it by
      Treating a fast "I don't know" differently from a slow one, and following it with "what would you try first?". That still counts if they really don't know (Baker's "gaming the system" research).
      Change to how Lotus reads evidence

      Confident and wrong, everywhere

      15

      "Very sure" on eight answers, three of them right.

      Answer-only marking sees
      Maths gaps only.
      Catch it by
      Measuring calibration (how well confidence matches correctness) as its own result for the teacher. Low performers tend to be overconfident, and that pattern stays put without help.

      Running out of steam

      16

      Sign slips start appearing in question 18 and not before.

      Answer-only marking sees
      A sign gap.
      Catch it by
      Putting the deciding questions early, watching for a late-test drop, and not naming a mistake from late answers alone. PISA finds items get harder as they appear later in a test, more so for less persistent students.
      Change to the question order

      The screen and the language typing, vocabulary, medium of instruction

      A typing problem, marked as maths

      17

      Types x+2(x+3) meaning (x + 2)(x + 3), or x2 for x².

      Answer-only marking sees
      A wrong factorisation.
      Catch it by
      Showing "here's what we read" before marking, and letting the student confirm. Typing slips stay separate from maths (the STACK approach: check what was typed before marking it).

      A vocabulary gap, not a maths gap

      18

      Asked to factorise, the student expands, every time. In a Hindi-medium class, "factorise" may never have been the word they used.

      Answer-only marking sees
      A wrong answer on every question.
      Catch it by
      Spotting "did the reverse operation" as its own pattern, adding a tap-to-see glossary in the student's medium, and keeping wording short. Wordy items measure language as well as maths (Abedi & Lord).
      Change to how questions are written

      The real gap is somewhere else foundations underneath factorising

      The gap is lower down

      19

      For x² + 11x + 28, writes (x + 3)(x + 8). The method is right, but they think 3 × 8 = 28.

      Answer-only marking sees
      A factorising gap.
      Catch it by
      Reading the arithmetic inside the wrong answer: a sum that matches with a product that's slightly off points to a times-table slip. Then probe one step down the skill map (knowledge-space theory, as used in ALEKS).
      Change to how Lotus reads evidence

      Letters as objects

      20

      Thinks 3a means "3 apples", so a + b can't be simplified further, and factorising feels like rearranging labels.

      Answer-only marking sees
      Random-looking algebra errors.
      Catch it by
      A short question family on what letters mean: "When is L + M + N = L + P + N true? Always, never, or sometimes?" (Küchemann's CSMS levels).
      New question family

      "=" means "do something"

      21

      Sees x² + 5x + 6 = (x + 2)(x + 3) and tries to solve for x, not seeing it's true for every x.

      Answer-only marking sees
      Nothing, until identities and equations get mixed up later.
      Catch it by
      "Is this true when x = 10? Why?" Students who see "=" as a relationship solve equations better (Knuth, Stephens, McNeil & Alibali 2006).
      New question family

      Can do it, can't see it

      22

      Factorises x² − 16 instantly, but expands (x + 3)² − 16 into a trinomial first. Works out 99² − 1 the long way.

      Answer-only marking sees
      Right answers, so no gap.
      Catch it by
      Questions where seeing the structure makes them short and missing it makes them long. Record the route taken (Hoch & Dreyfus's "structure sense").

      Knows one answer, not the whole set

      23

      For which b does x² + bx + 12 factorise? Finds 7, 8 and 13, and never thinks of −7, −8 or −13.

      Answer-only marking sees
      Three right answers.
      Catch it by
      "Find them all" questions. How complete the list is, and the order they found things in, show whether they understand the full set of answers or know a few examples.

      A different method, marked as a mistake

      24

      The class was taught splitting the middle term: x² + 5x + 6 = x² + 2x + 3x + 6. Lotus expects the product–sum shortcut, so the working looks off.

      Answer-only marking sees
      Odd working, or a false mistake.
      Catch it by
      Recognising every valid method students are taught (NCERT's included) and diagnosing within the method the student actually used.
      Change to marking

      The question itself broken, unfair, copied, or wasted

      The question is broken

      25

      An AI-written option that nobody ever picks, or a question that strong students get wrong more often than weak ones.

      Answer-only marking sees
      Normal results.
      Catch it by
      Checking question health after every class: options picked by fewer than 5% of students are dead (one medical-education study found about half of distractors were), and questions where strong students do worse get flagged for review.
      Quality check after each class

      The question is unfair to some students

      26

      English-medium and Hindi-medium students with the same overall result score very differently on one question.

      Answer-only marking sees
      A gap in one group.
      Catch it by
      Comparing students of equal overall ability across groups, a standard fairness check called differential item functioning. If the groups differ on one question, the wording is the problem, not the students.
      Quality check after each class

      Copied answers

      27

      Two neighbours with the same unusual wrong answers, in the same order.

      Answer-only marking sees
      Two students with the same gap.
      Catch it by
      Giving every student their own numbers (same skill, different instance). Copying stops helping, and identical answers to different questions stand out.
      Change to how questions are written

      The wasted question

      28

      Lotus is torn between "flips signs" and "knows it", then asks a question that both kinds of student answer the same way.

      Answer-only marking sees
      One more answer, and nothing learned.
      Catch it by
      Choosing each next question by how much it's expected to separate the explanations still in play, and stopping when Lotus is sure enough (Bayesian adaptive testing).

      Research behind this: Wise & Kong (2005) on response-time effort; Baker et al. on gaming intelligent tutors; Händel & Dresel, and Erickson & Heit, on overconfidence in maths; Debeer & Janssen and later PISA work on item-position effects; Sangwin's STACK on checking input before marking; Abedi & Lord (2001) on language load in maths items; Falmagne & Doignon's knowledge-space theory (ALEKS); Küchemann's CSMS study of what letters mean; Knuth et al. (2006) on the equal sign; Hoch & Dreyfus on structure sense; Tarrant et al. (2009) on non-functioning distractors; differential-item-functioning studies of language groups; and work on mutual-information question selection in diagnostic adaptive testing.

      How Lotus gets sharper: six changes

      All 28 blind spots come back to six parts of Lotus. Each part below lists what to change, in plain terms, and which blind spots it closes.

      1Record more than the answer

      • Keep the middle steps, not only the final text.
      • Keep the edit history, time to first action, total time and pauses.
      • Keep confidence or the chip split, take-backs and revisits.
      • Record whether the student confirmed "what we read" before marking.

      Closes 01, 05, 06, 13, 17. Mostly recording, with no new questions needed.

      2Read evidence more carefully

      • Set aside rapid guesses and typing problems before diagnosing.
      • Name a mistake only when it shows up again from a second angle.
      • Group wrong answers by the missing idea behind them.
      • Trace wrong arithmetic down to the prerequisite it points to.
      • Accept every valid taught method.

      Closes 07, 08, 13, 14, 19, 24.

      3Choose the next question by what it will reveal

      • Pick the question expected to best separate the explanations still in play, and stop when sure.
      • Cover every sign case before calling a skill secure.
      • Mix question types, and plant a look-alike now and then.
      • Ask the deciding questions early, before fatigue sets in.
      • Ask "what would you do first?" before giving any hint.

      Closes 02, 04, 09, 16, 28.

      4Write questions that separate students

      • Have code choose numbers so each mistake gives a different answer, with each option standing for one mistake.
      • Add near-miss twins, structure questions, "find them all" questions, and question families on what letters and "=" mean.
      • Use plain, short wording, with a glossary in the student's medium.
      • Vary the surface, and give every student their own numbers.

      Closes 03, 10, 11, 18, 20, 21, 22, 23, 27.

      5Check question health after every class

      • Track how often each option is picked, and replace dead ones.
      • Flag questions where strong students do worse than weak ones.
      • Run fairness checks by medium of instruction and by gender.
      • Send AI-written questions that misbehave to a review queue.

      Closes 25 and 26, and keeps every other fix honest over time.

      6Show the teacher five kinds of gap, not one

      • Maths misconception: "flips signs into brackets".
      • Foundation gap: "times tables to 12".
      • Language: "doesn't know the word factorise".
      • Confidence: "sure when wrong".
      • Engagement: "rushed the last third".

      Each needs a different response from the teacher. Lumping them together as "weak at factorising" hides all five.

      When to build each part

      Before the Dec 1 showcase

      Cheap: mostly recording and rules, no new data needed.

      • Rapid-guess filter (13)
      • "Here's what we read" before marking (17)
      • Record steps, edits and timing (01, 05, 06)
      • Every student gets their own numbers (27)
      • Sign-case coverage in the plan (02)
      • Bracket doors and Riya vs Sam in place of the two blind games

      During the pilot

      Needs real classes, but no long history.

      • Dead-option and broken-question report (25)
      • Prerequisite trace-back from wrong arithmetic (19)
      • Follow-up after a fast "I don't know" (14)
      • Confidence calibration in the teacher report (15)
      • Structure and "find them all" questions (22, 23)
      • The half-class comparison against today's questions

      After the pilot

      Needs data from many students to tune.

      • Next-best-question picker that replaces the fixed plan (28)
      • Fairness checks by medium and gender (26)
      • Grouping mistakes by missing idea, learned from real answers (08)
      • Letter-meaning and "=" question families (20, 21)
      • Recognising every taught method (24)

      Today a question records one thingThe final option the student picked. That's one data point, often a coin toss between "doesn't know" and "has a misconception".

      These record the student's pathWhere they hesitate, what they take back, how they split their certainty, and when they spot an error. The same minutes give several times the evidence.

      Still no scoreNothing on screen says right or wrong. The motion responds to what the student does (tiles snapping, chips landing, a lock opening), never to whether the answer was correct.
