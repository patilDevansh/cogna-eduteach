What today's questions miss

The games look different, but they all ask the same multiple-choice question

The games are decoration

Fireflies, critters and fish are drawn over an ordinary multiple-choice question. The maths the student does is the same with or without the game. The code says so itself: "These are skins only".

Different wrong answers record the same mistake

All three wrong critters in Spot the Impostor are logged as one mistake. Someone who checks only the first and last terms, and someone who is guessing, both pick a random critter. Lotus can't tell them apart.

lotus-probes.ts:129

Right answer, wrong reason

In Detective, a student who keeps every sign the same would write a different line 2. So they flag line 2, the correct answer, and get recorded as knowing it. Fireflies has the same problem. Its number pairs are right for a student who then flips the signs inside the brackets, which is one of the most common factorising mistakes.

Confidence is asked for but not used

On re-check turns the student says how sure they are. That goes to the AI as a hint, but the code that decides whether a gap is real never reads it. A confident wrong answer and a shrugging guess count the same.

What the research says

Seven rules for questions that diagnose

Every wrong answer points to one mistake. If two wrong options mean the same thing, one of them is wasted. A student who picks option A should have a different gap from one who picks C.

Eedi Diagnostic Questions (Craig Barton): each distractor maps to a single misconception, and you can't get it right while holding the key misconception.

Choose numbers that make mistakes show. Pick the instance so that each known mistake produces a different answer. Then the answer names the mistake.

Brown & Burton (1978), BUGGY: mistakes are small, consistent edits to the right procedure, so they can be predicted and tested for.

Ask how sure they are, and use it. A confident wrong answer suggests a misconception. An unsure wrong answer suggests they don't know yet. These two need different teaching.

Three-tier diagnostic tests: in one study the misconception rate fell from 56% on answers alone to 18% once reasons and confidence were added. Answer-only tests overestimate misconceptions.

Ask why, as a second choice. A short reason choice after the answer catches the student who is right by luck or by a broken rule.

Treagust's two-tier tests. The "correct answer trap" (Imran & Bulathwela 2026, Eedi data): flawed reasoning hidden behind correct answers.

Make the student find and fix errors. Spotting a wrong step and choosing its fix shows what the student thinks the right step is.

Booth, Lange, Koedinger & Newton (2013): incorrect examples reduce misconceptions in algebra.

Let them make their own example. "Build one that works" can't be guessed, and the example a student builds shows what they think the rule is.

Watson & Mason (2005), learner-generated examples: the examples a learner can build show how far their understanding reaches.

Make the game the maths. When the game action is the maths itself, children learn more and play longer. The path they take to the answer is extra evidence.

Habgood & Ainsworth (2011), Zombie Division: the integrated version gave more learning and 7× more voluntary play. PISA uses the path students take (log data) to tell apart strategies that end in the same answer.

The proof

Same skills, same simulated students: the new questions find the mistake about three times as often

I simulated 20,000 students. Each one holds a hidden state for each skill: they know it, they hold one specific mistake, or they don't know it yet. Every student makes careless slips 8% of the time. Today's questions are copied from the code, with the same numbers and options. For each option I worked out from the maths which students would pick it. Then I asked one question: after the answers, can Lotus name the mistake correctly?

Where today's questions go blind

Where the new formats lose. Today's one-step "Is it fully factorised?" question was already well built: each wrong option means one thing. It gives more information per minute than its two-step replacement (). The two-step version is worth it because it halves false alarms, not because it is faster. Asking the extra step on every question would cost time for little gain, so keep it to multiple-choice questions, where guessing is possible.

If the confidence assumption is wrong. The simulation assumes students with a misconception feel surer than students who are guessing, as the research reports. I re-ran it with that difference mostly removed. With one question per skill, the new formats still name the right mistake of the time, against for today's formats. Most of the gain comes from how the questions are built, not from the confidence tap.

What this does not prove yet. These are simulated students with clean, consistent mistakes, and real children are messier. The time per question is my estimate. The next step is a real comparison in a pilot class: half the class gets today's question in four slots and half gets the new one. Then check both against a short follow-up interview with each student. If the gap shrinks with real students, we learn which rule matters most.

Game design

What will work in a diagnostic, and what won't

Works

  • Building games where the build is the answer. The area grid, tile bridges and crossword. The order of tries tells Lotus about strategy.
  • Sorting and netting. Fishing gives several yes/no answers on one screen, so a real mistake shows up again and again while a slip shows up once. Keep it.
  • Two characters who disagree. "Riya says, Sam says" turns a check into a judgement. Students argue with characters more readily than with the test.
  • Make your own. Open enough to be fun, and it can't be guessed.
  • Calm, meaningful motion. Motion that marks evidence (the pond) or confirms a build (tiles snapping in). Never motion that marks right or wrong.

Won't work

  • Timers and speed rounds. These measure speed and stress, not understanding. Anxiety uses up working memory, and the students most affected are the ones we most need to read clearly.
  • Lives, points, streaks. Losing a life makes guessing feel safer than saying "I don't know". That swaps a clean "doesn't know yet" for noise.
  • A new skin on the same multiple-choice question. Fireflies and critters. Novelty wears off after a few questions, and the evidence is no better than before.
  • True / false. A 50% guess rate. One answer barely tells you anything.
  • Fiddly dragging on phones. A missed drop looks like a wrong answer. Use taps and pickers.
  • Long story questions. Reading load hides the maths gap we're trying to see.
  • Showing right or wrong during the check. It teaches during the test, so later answers stop being a clean reading.

If you want this in Lotus

A sensible order to build it in

  1. Fix the two blind games first. Replace Fireflies with bracket doors, where each wrong door is a different mistake. Replace Spot the Impostor with Riya vs Sam. Both are code-built and checked by the algebra engine, the same way the current probes are.
  2. Let confidence count in the gap rules. A confident wrong answer that matches a known mistake counts as strong evidence. An unsure one counts as "doesn't know yet" and never as a named misconception. This fits your recent change that stopped slips and guesses counting toward a gap.
  3. Add the "why?" step to multiple-choice slots only. Detective becomes find-and-fix, and the "Is it fully factorised?" question gets reasons.
  4. Swap the frog for the misty pond. Progress comes from how much the evidence has settled, so an adaptive test with an uncertain length still shows honest progress.
  5. Pilot the area grid and make-your-own as two new code-built formats, recording the path the student takes, not just the final answer.
  6. Run the class comparison described above before rolling out further.
